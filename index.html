How Australia Remembers

Over 100,000 Australians have died in war.
Do the nation's memorials reflect that cost?

Every year on 25 April, Australians mark Anzac Day. Crowds gather at war memorials in towns and cities. Some memorials list local names; others are a single stone.

But Australia's wars were not equally deadly. The two world wars took 101,385 lives between them. Vietnam took 524. Afghanistan took 47. So does each war get memorials to match its losses?

This story answers that question in four steps. It draws on Australian War Memorial and Queensland Government records.

  1. The cost of war: how many died in each war.
  2. What Australia builds to remember: the kinds of memorial.
  3. Where memorials stand: a closer look at Queensland.
  4. Do memorials match the cost? The answer, war by war.

1The cost of war

How many Australians died in each war?

103,139 in total, counted by the Australian War Memorial (AWM). Almost all of them died in the two world wars.

2What Australia builds to remember

How does Australia remember its wars?

With memorials of every kind, from honour boards to halls. Places of Pride, the AWM's national register, lists 12,935. Anyone can add an entry, so it keeps growing. This count is from 12 October 2026.

3Where memorials stand: Queensland

Why only Queensland? The national register cannot be downloaded as data. Queensland publishes its register, with locations, as open data. That makes it possible to map every memorial.

Where do Queensland's memorials stand, and what do they say?

Most stand in the south‑east and along the coast. Their words speak most of honour and memory. The state's register lists 1,399 memorials, up to December 2019.

761 of 1,399 memorials (54%) honour the First World War.

4Do memorials match the cost?

Across Australia, do memorials match each war's share of deaths?

No. The world wars are remembered less than their cost. Smaller wars, old and new, are remembered more.

Conclusion

Memory is spread more evenly than the deaths were

So, do Australia's memorials reflect the cost of its wars? Only partly.

The two world wars caused 98% of deaths, yet hold only 70% of memorial mentions. Smaller wars, old and new, are remembered far beyond their cost. Vietnam caused 0.5% of deaths but holds 8.7% of mentions.

Nearly half of all registered memorials are rolls, boards and plaques. In Queensland, memorials crowd the south‑east and the coast. Their inscriptions speak most of honour, memory and “Lest we forget”.

These counts show how often a war is remembered, not how important it was. Nor can they explain why communities chose to remember the way they did.

Each memorial is a choice made by one community, in its own way. Together, they show how Australia remembers: widely, but unevenly.

Key terms

Anzac Day
25 April, Australia's national day of war remembrance. ANZAC means Australian and New Zealand Army Corps.
Australian War Memorial (AWM)
Australia's national war museum and memorial, in Canberra.
Honour board, honour roll
A list naming people from a community who served.
Cairn, obelisk, plinth
A stone pile, a tall pointed pillar, a raised base.
Digger
Australian slang for a soldier; here, a soldier statue.
Cenotaph
A monument to the dead, who are buried elsewhere.
Malayan Emergency
The 1948–1960 conflict in Malaya, where Australian forces served.
Indonesian Confrontation
The 1962–1966 conflict over Malaysia's formation.
Council
Local government for a town, city or district.
RAAF
Royal Australian Air Force.
